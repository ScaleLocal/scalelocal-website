One filing season, not two

How the work fits together

For most of the owners we work with, the business return and the personal return are the same problem seen twice. A partnership files Form 1065 and an S corporation files Form 1120-S by 15 March; the schedule K-1 that comes out of that return is what makes the individual Form 1040, due 15 April, possible. Get the first date wrong and the second one is already in trouble — late 1065 and 1120-S penalties are charged per owner per month, for as long as twelve months.

The same is true in the other direction. What the business pays its owner drives the quarterly estimated instalments due 15 April, 15 June, 15 September and 15 January. Books that are closed monthly are what make those instalments a calculation rather than a guess. That is why the firm keeps bookkeeping, business filings and personal filings under one roof: the person who sees your ledger is in the same office as the person who signs your return.

Massachusetts is its own problem

A federal return prepared well is not automatically a Massachusetts return prepared well. The Commonwealth taxes most income at a flat rate and gives no standard deduction at all, so deductions that carried the federal return may do nothing here. It disallows federal bonus depreciation outright and has deferred conformity to the increased federal section 179 limits for the 2025 and 2026 tax years, which means a fixed-asset purchase can look very different on the two returns.

Extensions deserve particular care. The federal extension moves the filing date to 15 October and does not extend the time to pay. The Massachusetts six-month extension is automatic only if at least 80% of the total tax is already paid — fall short and the extension is void, with a late-filing penalty added on top.

Two credentials come up often, and they are not the same thing. An Enrolled Agent holds a federal credential granted by the IRS, earned by examination or by former IRS employment, carrying unlimited rights to represent taxpayers before the Service. A CPA is licensed by a state board and may additionally perform attest work. Both carry unlimited representation rights; a preparer with neither does not.
