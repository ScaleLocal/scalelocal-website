What to gather before a tax appointment

Guide

Most of the delay in a tax season is not the preparation itself — it is the second and third round of questions after a document turns up missing. Records can go into the client portal a few at a time.

Keep prior returns longer than feels necessary. The IRS normally has three years to assess additional tax, six where a return omits gross income exceeding 25% of the gross income stated, and no limit where none was filed.

Income

Every source, formed or not

The rule is every source, not every form. A form is how income gets reported, not what makes it reportable.

  • W-2s from each employer, including part-year jobs.
  • The 1099 family — interest, dividends, retirement distributions, Social Security, non-employee compensation, third-party network payments, state refunds, cancelled debt, unemployment. They arrive on different schedules, often long after the first ones.
  • Brokerage statements showing cost basis, not only proceeds. This is the most common gap: a sale reported with proceeds and no basis is treated as though the whole amount were gain until the basis is supplied, and reconstructing it later is slow work.
  • Schedule K-1s from partnerships, S corporations, estates and trusts. These often arrive last, and the return cannot be finished without them.
  • Rental income and expenses by property, and the date any improvement was placed in service.
  • Income that arrived with no form at all — cash work, a hobby that turned a profit, digital asset sales. No form does not mean no reporting obligation.

Deductions, credits and payments already made

Massachusetts has no standard deduction of its own and taxes most income at a flat 5%, so some of these work federally and some on the state return. Bring them all.

  • Mortgage interest and property tax statements, and the closing statement if you bought, sold or refinanced.
  • Tuition and student loan interest statements.
  • Childcare costs with the provider’s name, address and identification number — no credit without it.
  • Retirement and HSA contributions made outside payroll, with the year each counts toward.
  • Charitable receipts, and for a non-cash gift, what it was worth.
  • Health coverage forms, marketplace coverage especially.
  • Estimated payments with dates and amounts, federal and state separate. Guessing creates a mismatch that surfaces later as a notice.

The business file

Entity deadlines run ahead of the personal one: Forms 1065 and 1120-S on 15 March, Forms 1120 and 1040 on 15 April. Late partnership and S corporation penalties run per owner per month for up to twelve months.

Anything that changed

This is the category people skip, and the one that changes the return most. Mention it even if you are unsure it matters; deciding that is the accountant’s job.

  • A marriage, a divorce or a separation; a birth or adoption; a dependent who is no longer one.
  • A death in the household, including a parent whose final return is now yours.
  • A house bought or sold, or a home turned into a rental.
  • A move between states. Residents file Form 1, part-year residents and non-residents Form 1-NR/PY, and a mid-year move usually means more than one state return.
  • A business started, sold or closed; an inheritance or a distribution from an estate.
  • Retirement, a pension starting, or a rollover between accounts.
  • A first year of self-employment, which brings estimated payments.

More is better than less

Send the document you are unsure about. One that proves irrelevant costs a moment to set aside; one that never arrives costs an amended return, or a notice eighteen months later.

The same goes for gaps. If a K-1 will not arrive until September, if a year of books is unreconciled, if a return from three years ago was never filed — say so at the start. Each has a route through it, shorter when chosen early. A federal extension moves filing to 15 October but does not extend time to pay, and a Massachusetts extension holds only where 80% of the tax is paid.
