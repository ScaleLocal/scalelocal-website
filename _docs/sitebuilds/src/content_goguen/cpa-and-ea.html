CPA, EA, MST — what the letters actually mean

Guide

Almost anyone can prepare a tax return for money. That single fact explains why the letters after an accountant’s name matter, and why so few people know what they stand for. The two credentials you will meet most often — CPA and EA — come from different authorities, are earned in different ways, and are widely assumed to rank one above the other. They do not, at least not in the way most people think.

Thomas L. Fitzpatrick IV holds an MBA, an Enrolled Agent credential and a CPA licence. Brian D. Goguen holds a master’s in taxation and has been a licensed CPA since 1981. What follows is what each credential requires and what it lets the holder do for you.

Certified Public Accountant

A CPA is licensed by a state board of accountancy — here, Massachusetts. It is a state credential, not a federal one, and not a membership you can buy. Getting it takes a university education in accounting to a specified level, a uniform examination, and a period of documented work experience under supervision. All three must be satisfied before a licence is issued.

Keeping it takes more. A licensed CPA must complete continuing professional education on a recurring cycle and report it, and is bound by a written code of professional conduct covering independence, objectivity, integrity and confidentiality. That code has teeth: complaints are investigated, and a board can suspend or revoke a licence.

One thing only a CPA can do is attest work — audits, reviews and other engagements in which a firm expresses a formal opinion on financial statements that a bank, a regulator or a board will rely on. That is the clearest functional line between a CPA and any other tax credential.

Enrolled Agent

An Enrolled Agent holds a federal credential granted directly by the Internal Revenue Service. There is no state involvement and no state boundary: an EA credential earned in Massachusetts is the same credential everywhere else.

There are two routes to it. The usual one is a comprehensive examination administered on behalf of the IRS covering individual taxation, business taxation, and representation and procedure. The other is qualifying former employment with the IRS in a role applying and interpreting the tax code. Either way, the subject matter is taxation and nothing else.

That narrowness is the point. Where a CPA licence covers the whole discipline of accounting, the EA covers one field to the ground: the Internal Revenue Code, the regulations under it, and the procedures by which the IRS assesses, collects and disputes tax.

The part most people get wrong

The common assumption is that a CPA outranks an EA when it comes to dealing with the IRS. That is not how representation rights work. A CPA and an Enrolled Agent both hold unlimited rights to represent a taxpayer before the IRS — either one can speak for you in an examination, in collection, and on appeal, on any matter, whether or not they prepared the return. A preparer holding no credential does not have those rights.

So if the question is who can stand between you and an examiner, the two credentials are equal. Where they differ is elsewhere: only the CPA can perform attest work, and only the CPA answers to a state board.

MST, and the credentials that are not credentials

An MST is a Master of Science in Taxation — a specialist graduate degree in tax law and its application, not a general business degree. Brian holds both an MBA from Suffolk University and a master’s in taxation from Bentley College; only the second one is about tax. A specialist tax degree signals depth in areas such as entity structure and the interaction of federal and state rules — which in Massachusetts is not academic, since the state disallows federal bonus depreciation and has deferred conformity to the increased federal section 179 limits for the 2025 and 2026 tax years.

What the law actually requires

Anyone who prepares a federal return for compensation must have a preparer tax identification number from the IRS and must sign the return. That is the whole of the mandatory floor: no examination, no education requirement, no continuing education. Separately, the word “accountant” is not a protected title. “CPA” and “Enrolled Agent” are different — those are defined credentials, and they can be verified.

That is not a reason for alarm about whoever has done your return for years. It is a reason to know which category they fall into, because the category decides what happens if a notice arrives.

Worth asking before you engage anyone

  1. What credential do you hold, and who issued it? A licence or an enrolment can be verified.
  2. If the IRS or the Department of Revenue writes to me about this return, can you represent me — and is that included or billed separately?
  3. Who prepares the return, and who reviews it?
  4. Will you sign the return as paid preparer? A paid preparer is required to sign it and to give you a complete copy.
  5. What is your experience with my situation — a rental, a partnership, a non-profit, income in more than one state?
  6. How do you handle the e-file authorisation? You must sign it before the return can be transmitted.

The last one is the quiet test: a preparer who wants that signature before you have seen the finished return has the sequence backwards.
