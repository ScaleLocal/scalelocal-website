The returns prepared here

Business & personal

Corporations on Forms 1120 and 1120S. Partnerships on Form 1065. Single member LLCs on Schedules C and E of the owner’s own return. Non-profits on Form 990. Fiduciary returns on Form 1041. On the personal side, Form 1040 with every state filing it requires.

The last two on that business list are worth pausing over. A Form 990 for a youth league or an arts non-profit, and a Form 1041 for an estate or a trust, are both narrower work than a 1120-S, and decline the Form 1041 for the estate of a client who died in March. Both sit outside the ordinary rhythm of an owner-operated business, and both carry their own vocabulary — functional expense allocation and public support on the 990, distributable net income and the split between the entity and the beneficiaries on the 1041. Scott prepares them. If you have been passed along from one office to another because of a 990 or a 1041, that is the reason to call.

Massachusetts filings come with the federal return, not as an afterthought. Where you have moved during the year, or worked in one state and lived in another, the part-year and non-resident returns are part of the same job.

The entity return comes first

Order of work

A partnership or an S corporation does not pay the tax. It reports the result and hands each owner a Schedule K-1, and the tax is paid on the owner’s Form 1040. That means the personal return cannot be finished, or even honestly estimated, until the entity return is done. A 1040 filed on a guessed K-1 is a 1040 that will be amended.

The calendar reflects this. Forms 1065 and 1120-S are due 15 March; Forms 1120 and 1040 are due 15 April. The month between them exists so the K-1 can travel from one return to the other. When the books arrive in the last week of March, that month is already spent, and the extension that follows is not a delay in the filing so much as a delay in knowing the number.

Where Scott prepares both returns — which is the usual arrangement for owner-operated clients — the K-1 does not have to be requested, chased or interpreted. It moves across one desk.

What a late partnership or S corporation return costs

The penalty for filing a late Form 1065 or Form 1120-S is not a flat fee on the entity. It is charged per owner, per month, for up to twelve months. Two things follow from that. The first is that a four-partner partnership accrues the penalty four times as fast as a sole shareholder’s S corporation. The second is that the meter runs on months, so a return that is one day into a new month has bought a whole month of it.

This is the single cheapest thing to get right in the whole calendar. An extension is filed in minutes and stops the clock on the filing penalty. What it does not stop is the interest and the failure-to-pay exposure on money actually owed, which is a separate matter and a reason to have a figure in hand before the date rather than after.

Extensions

An extension extends the time to file. It does not extend the time to pay. Where an extension is filed, the aim is still to have a reasonable estimate paid by the original date.

What is needed, and what slows a return down

Before you send anything

For a business return: a closed set of books for the year, the bank and card statements behind them, the payroll reports and the W-2 and 1099 filings, loan statements showing the principal and interest split, a list of anything bought that has to be capitalised, and last year’s return with its depreciation schedule. For a personal return: the W-2s and 1099s, the K-1s, mortgage interest and property tax, tuition and student loan interest, brokerage statements with cost basis, and the closing statement on anything sold.

The four things that make a return take far longer than it should

  • Books that are not closed. A trial balance that still moves is a return that has to be prepared twice.
  • Missing basis. A stock sale or a property sale without the purchase figure stops the return until it is found.
  • Owner draws and business spending run through the same account, so the personal and the business sides have to be separated before anything can be totalled.
  • Documents arriving in pieces over six weeks rather than in one delivery.

None of this requires perfection. It requires everything at once.
