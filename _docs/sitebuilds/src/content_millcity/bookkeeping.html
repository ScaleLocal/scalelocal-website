What a reconciled month actually tells you

Compilation

A bank reconciliation is the point at which the books stop being an opinion. Every deposit is matched to something sold, every payment to something bought, and whatever is left over is investigated rather than absorbed. Done monthly it takes an hour or two. Done once a year it takes a week, and half the answers are gone — nobody remembers what the transfer in June was for, and the owner is guessing about his own money.

The return is one output of that work, and it is the one owners think they are paying for. It is not the useful part. The useful part is that early in the month an owner can see what the last month cost him and whether the margin moved. A margin that fell in February and is found the following March is a fact about history.

It also produces a balance sheet, which answers what the profit and loss cannot: how much of the cash in the account is tax owed to the Commonwealth, and whether the card balance is growing while the income statement looks fine.

The chart of accounts is the real design decision

Set once

Every bookkeeping question that matters is settled by the chart of accounts, usually by whoever clicked through the software setup in the first week of trading. The default list is built for no particular business, and it will happily produce a tidy, reconciled, entirely uninformative set of accounts for years.

A chart of accounts that has been thought about does three things. It maps onto the lines of the return that will eventually be filed. It separates what the owner needs to manage — food cost apart from paper goods, repairs apart from improvements, one property apart from another. And it holds money collected for someone else in a liability account rather than in income.

Too few accounts and the books say nothing. Too many and nothing is posted consistently, which is worse: inconsistent detail looks like information.

A quick-serve restaurant

The books run daily and the source document is the point-of-sale close, not the bank. Sales by category, meals tax to its own liability, comps and voids on separate lines, cash over and short recorded the same day. Delivery is reconciled from the platform statement, because the deposit arrives net of commission and booking it as sales buries the fee. Meals tax is filed monthly on Form ST-MAB-4, by every vendor, with no volume threshold. See restaurants.

A landlord

Nothing happens daily and everything is per unit. Schedule E asks for each property in its own column, so the accounts are built that way from the start. Deposits sit as a liability until applied. Mortgage payments are split into principal and interest. Improvements are scheduled as they occur — date, cost, what was done — because that schedule sets depreciation and, decades later, the gain on sale. See rentals.

Books and the return are one job in two parts

An accountant handed a shoebox in March is doing bookkeeping under time pressure, with no way to ask a question that still has an answer. An accountant who reconciled the year as it went along is preparing a return from records he already trusts. Same job, done either in twelve unhurried pieces or one hurried one.

Compilation work here is monthly, quarterly or annual. The cadence follows the business: payroll and meals tax pull towards monthly, and a landlord with four units and no employees is often fine on quarterly.
