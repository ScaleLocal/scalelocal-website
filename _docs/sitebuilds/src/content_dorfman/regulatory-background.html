The background

What a regulator’s eye buys a small business

Estee C. Dorfman, CPA, MSA was previously employed by the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority — formerly known as the National Association of Securities Dealers — as a Principal Examiner. She co-founded Dorfman & Dorfman, CPAs in 2008 with Marvin H. Dorfman, CPA.

Most business owners in Wilmington have never had occasion to hear of that authority, and there is no reason they should have. It oversees securities firms, not landscapers or dentists or machine shops. So the fair question from a client is not “how impressive” but “what does that do for me?”

Start with what the work is. An examiner reads records and decides whether they support what has been asserted. Someone has stated a figure — a balance, a total, a date. The examiner asks what evidence stands behind it and then reads the evidence. That is the whole of it.

It is worth saying what that work is not. It is not suspicion. It is verification, and the ordinary result of verification is that the number holds.

Four habits

What carries over into ordinary accounting

Years of asking one question leaves habits. Four of them matter to a small business, and none are exotic.

01Documentation made at the timeA record written when the transaction happened carries weight. The same record reconstructed eighteen months later from memory and a credit card statement carries much less, and everyone reading it can tell the difference.Contemporaneous
02Reconciliation to an outside statementInternal consistency is easy and proves little. Books that agree with a bank statement, a loan schedule or a payroll register have been checked against something the business did not write.Outside evidence
03A clear line between business and personA separate account, a separate card, and an owner’s draw recorded as a draw. Where the line blurs, every figure downstream becomes arguable, and the argument comes at the worst moment.Separation
04Being able to answer “how do you know?”Every figure should have a short, boring answer describing where it came from. If the honest answer is that it was estimated, the file should say so rather than let a reader assume otherwise.Provenance

None of this makes the bookkeeping slower in any way that shows up on an invoice. It changes what the records are capable of, later.

When it is tested

The file is built years before anyone reads it

Records sit untouched for long stretches and then, without much warning, someone reads them closely. The occasions differ, but the request is nearly always the same: show the support for this number.

OccasionWhat gets asked for
A notice from the IRS or the Department of RevenueThe support behind one line on one return
An examination of a filed returnSubstantiation for a deduction or a basis figure
A lender’s diligence before creditStatements that tie to the tax returns and the bank
A buyer’s diligence on a saleEvidence that the earnings are the company’s, not the owner’s
A divorceA defensible line between household and business
An insurance claimProof of what existed, and of what it cost

Two of those readers are friendly and four are not, and it makes no difference to the outcome. The file either supports the number or it does not, and that was decided years earlier — on the day the record was made, or the day it was not.

The relevant periods are long. A return is generally open to assessment for three years; six where it omits gross income exceeding 25% of the gross income stated; and without limit where no return was filed. Records kept for the length of a busy season are not kept long enough.

Scope stated honestly

What the same discipline requires the firm to say

The habit of asking what a record supports applies to the accountant’s own work, and it cuts against overstatement. An engagement means something specific, and a client is entitled to know which one they have bought.

A real constraint

Independence is required for assurance work. An accountant who keeps the books for a business ordinarily cannot also provide assurance on those same books. This is a limit the firm will state up front rather than discover late.

The firm offers review and compilation of small business financial statements, and bookkeeping services to trial balance. Those are different levels of work at different cost, explained on the review and compilation page. Payroll work here means payroll tax returns, federal and state; nothing broader is implied.

Naming the boundary is not modesty. It is the same reflex as documenting the transaction: put it in writing at the time, so nobody has to reconstruct it later.
