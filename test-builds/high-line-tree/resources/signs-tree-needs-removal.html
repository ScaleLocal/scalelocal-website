7 Signs Your Tree Needs to Be Removed

Updated April 2026 6 min read By Bobby Black, ISA Certified Arborist

When a Tree Becomes a Problem

Not every problem tree needs to come down. But some do. After 18 years of assessing trees across Massachusetts, I've learned to spot the ones that are genuinely unsafe. This list covers the situations where removal is usually the right answer.

The tricky part? Some signs are obvious. Others are subtle. A tree can look healthy from the driveway but be failing internally. That's why I always recommend getting a certified arborist to evaluate a tree you're worried about. It's better to know.

Sign #1: Significant Foliage Loss Outside of Dormancy

1

A tree that's dropping leaves in the middle of summer or early fall (when it should still be green and full) is telling you something is wrong. This isn't the natural shedding that happens in October and November—this is premature, sporadic leaf loss across the crown.

Causes can range from disease to root damage to insect damage. Either way, if a tree is that stressed, it's struggling. I often recommend removal if more than 50% of the foliage is missing and the tree isn't recovering year to year.

Sign #2: Peeling Bark, Cankers, or Cavities

2

Run your hand along the trunk. If bark is sloughing off in chunks, or if you see large cankers (sunken areas with dead bark), that's a problem. Deep cavities in the trunk—actual holes or hollowed-out sections—mean the tree's structural integrity is compromised.

A cavity doesn't always mean the tree has to come down immediately. But it does mean the tree is weaker and less able to withstand wind or snow load. Combined with other signs, it often tips the scales toward removal.

Sign #3: Fungal Growth at the Base

3

Mushrooms or shelf fungi growing from the base or trunk aren't there for decoration—they're decomposing the wood. These fungi indicate internal rot. The visible fruiting bodies (the mushrooms you see) are just the fruiting structure of much larger decay happening inside the tree.

If you see fungi at the base, especially on a large tree, that tree is likely hollow or heavily compromised inside. This is a serious warning sign that removal should be considered soon.

Sign #4: Sudden Lean (Not a Natural Lean)

4

Some trees naturally lean slightly. That's fine. But a tree that develops a new, noticeable lean—especially after a storm—is often warning you that the root system is damaged or compromised. A tree that's suddenly tilting is unstable.

I've seen trees develop severe leans and fail months later when another wind storm came through. A sudden lean is not something to wait on. Get it assessed immediately.

Sign #5: Major Storm Damage

5

When a tree loses more than 50% of its crown (branches), or when the trunk is split with a major fracture running up the wood, removal is usually the best option. The tree's going to struggle to recover, and you're left with a weakened, hazardous tree for years.

I'd rather remove a tree and plant a new one than nurse along a severely damaged tree that's been structurally compromised. Sometimes the most honest thing to do is let it go.

Sign #6: Dead or Hanging Branches (Widow-Makers)

6

Dead branches hanging in the crown are called "widow-makers" for a reason. They can drop without warning, especially during wind or ice storms. One branch through a car windshield or someone's head, and you've got a serious liability.

If a tree has numerous dead branches spread throughout the crown, it's declining. Removal is safer than gambling on which branches might fall when. If it's just a few dead branches, pruning can solve it. But when the tree's full of dead wood, that's a different story.

Sign #7: Root Damage from Construction, Compaction, or Grade Changes

7

If your property's been excavated, graded, or had soil added or removed around a tree's root zone, that tree's often in trouble. Root damage doesn't always show up immediately—sometimes trees take years to decline after root damage—but when it does, it's serious.

A tree with its roots damaged can't absorb water efficiently. It'll decline slowly, become more susceptible to disease, and eventually fail. These trees often need to come down.

The Scratch Test: A Quick Home Inspection

Here's something you can do right now if you're worried about a tree. Get a small knife and gently scratch the bark on a lower branch. Look at the color underneath.

If you see green, the branch is alive. If the wood underneath the bark is brown or gray and dry, that branch is dead. If multiple branches fail the scratch test, your tree's in trouble.

When a Tree CAN Be Saved

Not everything on this list automatically means removal. A tree with one or two dead branches? Prune them off and the tree moves on. A tree that's been topped or damaged but still has 60% of its canopy and good structure? Often it can recover with proper pruning and care.

But when you've got multiple warning signs—significant foliage loss plus cankers plus fungal growth—that's when removal becomes the realistic choice. A tree like that's never going to be healthy again.

Special Note: Emerald Ash Borer in Massachusetts

If you have ash trees, you should know this: the emerald ash borer has been detected in over 280 communities across Massachusetts. It's a destructive pest that kills ash trees within 3–5 years of infestation. Most ash trees in infested areas will die.

If you have an ash tree and you haven't treated it with preventive insecticide, I'd strongly recommend getting it assessed. Waiting until the tree's infested makes removal more expensive and more urgent.

My Philosophy on Tree Removal

I'd rather save a tree than remove it. Every time. A mature tree provides shade, reduces energy costs, increases property value, and supports wildlife. I want to keep them.

But I've also learned that some trees become liabilities. Waiting to remove a dangerous or dying tree doesn't make it cheaper—it makes it more hazardous and more expensive when it fails unexpectedly on your roof or deck. I'd rather recommend removal honestly than pretend a failing tree is going to somehow recover.
