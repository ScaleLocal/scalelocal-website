How Much Does Tree Removal Cost in Massachusetts?

Updated April 2026 7 min read By Bobby Black, ISA Certified Arborist

What You'll Actually Pay for Tree Removal

I get asked this question almost every week: "How much does tree removal cost?" The answer I usually give is: it depends. But I know that's not helpful when you're standing in your yard looking at a problem tree and need to know if you should budget $500 or $5,000.

After 18 years of removing trees across Massachusetts, I've handled hundreds of removals in our Merrimack Valley area. The truth is, pricing varies dramatically based on factors most homeowners don't think about. Let me break down what actually drives the cost.

Tree Size is Your Primary Cost Driver

The biggest factor in removal cost is tree size. I measure this by height because that's what determines the complexity and risk of the job.

Small Trees (Under 30 feet)

These typically cost $300–$700 to remove. A small white birch or ornamental maple falls into this category. The work is straightforward: climb up, section out the branches, lower them to the ground, and fell the trunk. Minimal equipment needed, shorter work time.

Medium Trees (30–60 feet)

Expect $800–$1,800. Most mature oaks, maples, and pines in residential yards fall here. These require more careful rigging, proper rope technique, and sometimes a crane truck depending on where the tree is located. This is where labor and equipment costs really add up.

Large Trees (60 feet and taller)

These run $2,000–$5,000 or more. Removing a 100-foot white pine near your house isn't a quick operation. We're talking multiple days sometimes, specialized equipment, and serious insurance considerations. I've priced removals in this range that went significantly higher when the tree was wrapped around power lines or hanging over a neighbor's pool.

Location Matters More Than You'd Think

Where a tree sits on your property dramatically affects the price. A tree in an open yard with plenty of fall space? That's straightforward work. But throw in complications, and costs rise fast.

Proximity to structures: If a tree is close to your house, garage, deck, or pool, the removal becomes much more complex. We have to rig each section down carefully to avoid damage. Nobody wants a $50,000 insurance claim to replace a roof because a tree section fell wrong. This can add 50–100% to the cost.

Power lines: Trees touching or near power lines require coordination with the utility company and sometimes specialized bucket trucks. This adds significant cost and coordination time.

Access for equipment: Can our truck get close to the tree? Does your driveway have to be protected? Are there fences we need to work around? Limited access means longer job times and potentially higher labor costs.

The Condition of the Tree

A healthy, living tree and a dead tree of the same size can have different removal costs—though not always in the direction people expect.

Dead trees are often more expensive to remove, not less. Here's why: dead wood is brittle. Branches snap unexpectedly. The trunk can splinter as you cut. You have to move slower, be more careful, and sometimes work takes twice as long. Plus, dead trees are the ones most likely to drop branches without warning, making them urgent removals that cost more.

A healthy tree with good branch structure is often easier and faster to remove. I can climb safely, cut confidently, and move through the work efficiently.

Stump Grinding: The Add-On Nobody Plans For

I've removed the tree. Now what about the stump? You have options:

  • Grind it down: $175–$450 depending on stump size and accessibility. Our grinder makes short work of most stumps.
  • Leave it for you: Much cheaper upfront, but you'll want it gone eventually (roots keep spreading, it's a tripping hazard, it limits what you can plant there).
  • Treat it with herbicide: Slows regrowth but doesn't eliminate the stump—sometimes a temporary solution.

Most of my customers bundle the grinding with the removal. It's convenient and you're not dealing with a 3-foot stump for the next 10 years.

When to Save Money: Seasonal Timing

Tree removal cost varies by season, and understanding this can save you hundreds.

Winter is the sweet spot. February and March are the slowest months for tree work—fewer storms mean fewer emergency calls hogging our crew's time. We can offer better rates. Plus, frozen ground means less damage to your lawn, and dormant trees are lighter (less moisture in the wood). Winter is genuinely the best time if you have time to wait.

Spring and summer are peak demand. Storms happen, trees fail, everyone wants their trees trimmed before summer. That means longer wait times and higher prices. If you don't have an emergency, I'd recommend scheduling for winter.

Will Your Homeowners Insurance Cover It?

This is important. Most Massachusetts homeowners insurance policies cover tree removal if the tree was damaged by a covered peril—usually a storm or lightning strike.

Typical coverage: $500–$1,000 per tree. If your removal falls under your insurance, you'll have a deductible ($500–$1,000 typically), but the insurance should cover the rest. Trees that are diseased, infested, or just "in the way" don't qualify for insurance coverage.

I often recommend customers file their insurance claim. It's what you're paying for. Just photograph everything and document the damage before anything gets moved.

Certified Arborist vs. General Contractor

This matters more than a lot of people realize. In Massachusetts, almost anyone can cut down a tree. You don't need a license. That's the problem.

A certified arborist has formal training in tree biology, proper pruning and removal techniques, and continues education. I'm ISA certified—that means I've passed rigorous exams and keep my knowledge current. When I remove a tree, I'm thinking about the impact on surrounding trees, proper disposal of the wood, and safety protocols that protect you and your neighbors.

A general contractor with a chainsaw might be cheaper. They might also leave you with property damage, hurt a neighbor's tree, or skip proper cleanup. I've been hired multiple times to clean up other contractors' removal jobs. That always costs more in the end.

My Approach: Honest Assessment

Here's something I pride myself on: I don't always recommend removal. I'd rather save a tree than cut it down. If a tree is declining but still viable, we can often prune it back, remove dead wood, and give it years of healthy life. That costs less than removal and you get to keep a mature tree on your property.

But when a tree is genuinely unsafe—hollow trunk, major structural failure, root damage from construction—removal is the right call. Waiting doesn't make it cheaper. It makes it more dangerous and often more expensive when the tree fails unexpectedly.

Getting an Accurate Estimate

Any legitimate tree service will give you an on-site estimate. They need to see the tree, assess its condition, understand your property layout, and check for hazards. Phone estimates are rough at best. In-person estimates are free—that's the standard in our industry.

A good estimate should include:

  • Tree height and diameter
  • Location on the property and proximity to structures
  • Cost for removal
  • Cost for stump grinding (if included)
  • Debris cleanup and disposal
  • Liability insurance details

If something seems off or the price is shockingly cheap, trust your gut. Ask questions. A professional will be happy to explain their pricing.

Final Word

Tree removal in Massachusetts isn't cheap, but it's not arbitrary either. You're paying for expertise, equipment, insurance, and the safe disposal of heavy wood. For a small tree, you might spend $500. For a large tree near your house, you might spend $3,000–$4,000.

The best move? Call early when you notice a problem. Get it assessed before it becomes an emergency. And don't hesitate to ask your tree service why something costs what it does. We're happy to explain.
