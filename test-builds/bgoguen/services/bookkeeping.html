Bookkeeping.

The monthly discipline underneath everything else, and the thing that decides whether a return takes three hours or three days.

What a month closed properly is worth

Bookkeeping

Most small businesses discover the state of their books in February, when the return is being prepared and the year can no longer be changed. A bank feed that has been importing without anyone reading it is not a set of books. It is a pile of transactions with guesses attached.

Closing a month means the bank and credit card balances agree with the statements, every transaction has been coded to an account someone chose deliberately, and anything unclear has been asked about while the answer is still fresh. Done that way, the books do work the return alone never does: they tell you what a job actually cost, whether the price is holding, what is owed and to whom, and how much of the cash in the account is already spoken for.

They also make the return cheap to produce. A year of clean months gets prepared. A year of unclean months is a reconstruction project finished under deadline pressure, with little room to think about anything but getting it filed.

The chart of accounts is the real decision

Software is not the hard part. The firm works in QuickBooks, which is where most of our clients already are, and the mechanics of it are learnable. What determines whether a set of books is useful is the chart of accounts — the list of categories every dollar has to land in.

A default chart is built for no business in particular. It tends to produce a profit and loss statement with one enormous line called something like “general expense” and no way to answer the question the owner actually has. Built for the business, the same statement separates the costs that move with revenue from the costs that do not, breaks out the two or three lines the owner watches, and keeps what the return needs — meals, vehicle, owner health insurance, depreciable assets — in its own place instead of buried.

That design is worth doing once, early, with the tax treatment in view. Changing it three years in means restating the comparatives or losing them.

A chart of accounts is a reporting decision and a tax decision at the same time.

Books and the return are one job in two parts

Why it runs together

When the same firm keeps the books and files the return, questions get asked in July rather than found in March. Fixed assets are set up as they are bought, so depreciation is not reverse-engineered from receipts — which matters in Massachusetts, where federal bonus depreciation is disallowed and conformity to the increased federal section 179 limits has been deferred for the 2025 and 2026 tax years. The two sets of figures diverge by design, and tracking that as it happens is far easier than rebuilding it later.

The calendar cooperates too. Partnership and S corporation returns are due 15 March, ahead of the 15 April individual deadline, because the owners cannot finish their own returns until the K-1s exist. Books closed monthly make that sequence work; books opened in February make it a scramble. The late-filing penalty on a Form 1065 or 1120-S is charged per owner per month for up to twelve months.

A quiet year still has a filing

A Massachusetts corporation owes a minimum corporate excise even in a loss year — $456 for the 2025 tax year. Slow trading is not a reason to let the books lapse; the return still has to be prepared and the figures still have to come from somewhere.

How it works in practice

Documents and questions move through the client portal rather than email, so a year of correspondence stays in one place. Each month is reconciled and closed, and you get the statements that come out of it — profit and loss, balance sheet, and whatever else the business needs to watch.
