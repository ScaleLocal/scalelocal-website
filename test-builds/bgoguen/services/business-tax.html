Small business and non-profit tax.

How a business is organized decides which return it files and when — and a non-profit return is a public document, which changes how the year should be kept.

The return follows the entity

Schedule C · 1065 · 1120-S · 1120

A small business does not pick its tax return from a menu. The return follows from how the business is organized and from elections already made. A sole proprietor or single-member LLC reports on Schedule C inside the owner’s personal Form 1040. A partnership or multi-member LLC files Form 1065. A corporation that has elected S status files Form 1120-S; one that has not files Form 1120 and pays tax in its own name. Nothing about the trade itself decides this — two contractors doing identical work can file entirely different returns.

What changes with the form is more than arithmetic: when the return is due, who bears the tax, what the Commonwealth charges on top, and how expensive a late filing becomes.

Each date moves to the next business day on a weekend or holiday. A federal extension moves filing to 15 October and does not extend the time to pay.

March comes before April for a reason

A Form 1065 or an 1120-S does not pay tax itself. It divides the year’s income among the owners and reports each owner’s share on a Schedule K-1, and that K-1 is an input to a personal return. Until the entity return is finished, no owner’s Form 1040 can be finished honestly — which is why the entity deadline sits a month ahead, and why a business return that slips does not slip alone.

The penalties make the point sharply. On an individual return, failure to file costs 5% of unpaid tax per month and failure to pay costs 0.5% per month, both capped at 25%; filing late costs ten times what paying late costs. A late Form 1065 or 1120-S works differently. It is charged per owner per month, for up to twelve months, whether or not the entity owes a cent. Four owners means the meter runs four times as fast, on a return that may show a loss.

Estimated installments run alongside this, due 15 April, 15 June, 15 September and 15 January. What the business pays its owner makes them a calculation rather than a guess.

Keep records longer than the season

Three years to assess normally, six where a return omits gross income exceeding 25% of the gross income stated, and no limit where no return was filed.

Two places Massachusetts parts company with the federal return

A loss year still costs something

A Massachusetts corporation owes a minimum corporate excise even in a year it loses money — $456 for the 2025 tax year. A corporation that had a hard year still files, and still writes a check.

Depreciation does not carry across

Massachusetts disallows federal bonus depreciation, and has deferred conformity to the increased federal section 179 limits for the 2025 and 2026 tax years. A truck or a machine written off aggressively on the federal return may be recovered over years on the Massachusetts one. The asset then needs two depreciation schedules side by side, and the gap follows it for the life of the asset, including the year it is sold.

Non-profits, and why a 990 is different

Exempt organizations

Exempt organizations are a stated part of what the firm does. Small non-profits are often awkward to place: too specialized for a preparer who works only on individual returns, too small to interest a firm built around audit engagements. The work sits here beside the partnerships and S corporations.

Form 990 is the annual information return through which an exempt organization reports to the IRS — what came in, what went out, who governs the organization, how key people are compensated, and what the programs did with the money. It is not a tax return in the ordinary sense, since the organization is generally not taxed on its exempt activities; it is the return by which exemption is maintained.

And it is a public document. Donors read a 990, and so do grantmakers assessing an application, prospective board members, and occasionally a local reporter. No other return the firm prepares is written for an audience. Keeping the year with that in mind means:

  • Program, administrative and fundraising costs classified consistently as they are incurred, not sorted retrospectively into whatever ratio the year happened to produce
  • Restricted gifts tracked apart from unrestricted ones from the day they arrive, with the restriction recorded in writing
  • Board minutes, the conflict-of-interest policy and compensation decisions documented when they happen — the return asks about all three
  • In-kind contributions valued and recorded when they are received

A 990 assembled from a box of paper can be perfectly accurate and still read badly. One built from books kept all year reads like an organization that knows what it is doing.
