Home/Start where you are/Two states in one year

A move into or out of Massachusetts.

Two states in one year, or living in one and working in another. Apportionment is the whole exercise, and it is one of the more common reasons a return has to be amended.

Which return

Residency for the year decides the form.

A full-year Massachusetts resident files Form 1. A part-year resident or a non-resident files Form 1-NR/PY.

A part-year resident reports income earned while resident in Massachusetts, plus Massachusetts-source income from the remainder of the year. A non-resident reports Massachusetts-source income only. Both require the year to be split, and the split is where the work is.

Apportionment

Splitting the year.

Wages are generally apportioned by where the work was performed rather than by where the employer sits or where the payslip was issued. Investment income usually follows residence at the time it was received. A capital gain follows residence at the date of sale, which means the timing of a large sale relative to a move can change which state taxes it — a fact worth knowing before the move rather than after.

Deductions and exemptions are generally prorated to the Massachusetts portion of the year, so the state figure is not simply a fraction of the federal one.

Keep the dates

The single most useful record here is a clear note of when the move happened and, where the work was performed in more than one state, how many days were worked where. Reconstructing that a year later from memory is the part that goes wrong.

Taxed twice

The credit is meant to prevent it, and it usually does.

Where two states tax the same income, the state of residence generally allows a credit for tax paid to the other state. It is a credit rather than an exemption, so it is limited to what the residence state would itself have charged on that income — if the other state charges more, the excess is not recovered.

Both returns have to be prepared in the right order for the credit to compute correctly, and the non-resident return generally has to be done first. Getting the order wrong is a common cause of an amended return.

Massachusetts rates

Massachusetts taxes most income — wages, interest, dividends and long-term capital gains — at a flat 5%. Short-term capital gains are taxed at a higher rate of their own. An additional 4% surtax applies to taxable income above a threshold a little over one million dollars, adjusted annually, and it reaches one-off events, so the year of a business sale or a property sale is the year to check it. There is no state standard deduction.
