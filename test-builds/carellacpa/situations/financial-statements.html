Someone has asked for financial statements.

A lender, a landlord, a bonding company or a prospective buyer. The first question is not how fast — it is which level of service they actually require.

Four levels

The word “statements” covers four different jobs.

They differ in how much work stands behind them, and therefore in what they cost and how long they take. Requests routinely arrive without specifying which is meant, and the gap between the cheapest and the dearest is very wide.

LevelWhat the accountant doesWhat it gives the reader
PreparationAssembles the statements from your recordsNo report, and no assurance
CompilationThe same assembly, plus a reading for obvious problems, plus a signed reportA report, but still no assurance
ReviewAnalytical procedures and enquiryLimited assurance
AuditTesting, confirmation and evidence gatheringAn opinion

A great many requests that arrive asking for “audited accounts” are satisfied by a compilation once somebody asks the question. Establishing that first is usually the largest single saving available in this whole exercise.

Ask what they will accept

The person who asked can nearly always tell you which level their credit policy requires, and the answer is frequently less than the phrasing of the request implied. It is a two-minute question that occasionally saves a great deal.

What is needed

Statements are built out of the books.

Which means the condition of the books decides the timetable. What has to be in place first:

  • Bank and credit card accounts reconciled through the period end
  • A chart of accounts that reflects how the business actually works, rather than whatever the software proposed on installation
  • Loans stated at their real balances, with the interest split out
  • A current depreciation schedule
  • Nothing significant sitting in a suspense or uncategorised account
  • Accounts receivable and payable that agree to something

Where the books are behind, the honest sequence is to bring them current first. Statements produced from unreconciled records are worth very little and a lender’s analyst tends to notice.

Timing

Deadlines that arrive with the request.

Requests of this kind usually carry a date, and the date is usually somebody else’s: a credit committee, a closing, a bonding renewal, a lease signature. The useful thing to establish on the first call is what the date actually is and what happens if it moves, because that determines whether the books can be brought current properly or whether the work has to be sequenced around a fixed point.

If a covenant in an existing loan agreement is what is driving the request, the agreement itself usually specifies the level of service and the deadline. It is worth reading before the work is scoped rather than after.
