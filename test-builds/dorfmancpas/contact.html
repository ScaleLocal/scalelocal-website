Arranging A Time

Arranging a time

A time is arranged in one of two ways. Telephone (781) 780-7069 and dial extension 11 once the call connects, or write to estee@dorfman-cpas.com and propose a few windows that work for you. The time is settled in that exchange, by a person.

There is no online booking tool, no calendar to click through and no scheduling link. This site holds no diary and cannot reserve anything on the firm’s behalf.

Email suits a short, specific question — a due date, whether a particular return is in scope, what a notice appears to be asking for. The telephone suits anything where scope has to be settled: what records exist, what condition they are in, and what level of service the outcome calls for.

If the matter is time-sensitive — a notice with a response date, or a deadline inside the next fortnight — say so in the first line, by either route.

The First Call

What a first call covers

A first call is short and factual. It establishes what you need, whether the firm does that work, and what it takes.

  1. Who is filing. An individual, a trust or estate, a corporation, a partnership or a limited liability company. The answer sets the form and the due date.
  2. What the work is. Tax returns, bookkeeping to trial balance, payroll tax returns, or a review or compilation of financial statements. The six services the firm advertises are listed, with their scope, on the services page.
  3. What already exists. A prior-year return, a set of books, a spreadsheet, or nothing yet.
  4. What the deadline is. Calendar-year Forms 1065 and 1120-S are due 15 March; Forms 1120 and 1040 are due 15 April, each moving to the next business day when the date falls on a weekend or a holiday.
  5. What happens next. Whether the firm takes the engagement, and what it needs from you before it starts.

If the work is outside what the firm does, that is said on the call rather than discovered later.

Before You Dial

What to have in front of you

None of this is required, and a call without any of it is still useful. It simply shortens the second one.

  • The most recent return filed, whichever form it was.
  • For an entity: the state of formation, the tax year end, and how it is currently treated for federal tax purposes.
  • Any notice received, with the date on it and the response date it states.
  • Where the bookkeeping lives now, and who has been doing it.
  • For a financial statement request: who is asking, and what level they asked for. Preparation, compilation and review are different engagements at different cost, and the party requesting usually specifies. The distinctions are set out on the review and compilation page.

Common questions are answered on the questions page, which may save the call entirely.

Sending Information

What not to put in an email

Ordinary email is not encrypted in transit in any way you can rely on. Social Security numbers, employer identification numbers, bank and brokerage account numbers, and images of complete returns should not be sent in an unencrypted email, attached or pasted into the body.

An email describing the situation in general terms carries no such risk. Write that, then ask on the call which route makes sense for the documents themselves — post, fax to (781) 780-7062, delivery to the office, or something else agreed between you.

On signatures

Under IRS e-file rules the taxpayer must sign the authorization before an electronic return originator may transmit the return. A paid preparer signs it as well and gives the taxpayer a complete copy. A return is not transmitted on a verbal go-ahead.

Ask first, send second. It costs one question and removes the guesswork.
