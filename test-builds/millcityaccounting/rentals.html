Home/Rental property
One of two specialisms

Rental property.

Schedule E, a depreciation schedule that has to survive twenty-seven and a half years, losses that may not be deductible in the year you incur them, and a recapture bill waiting at the sale.

What goes on Schedule E

Form 1040

Part I of Schedule E reports income and expenses from rental real estate. Each property gets its own column: rent received, then the expenses set against it — insurance, mortgage interest, management fees, repairs, supplies, taxes, utilities, depreciation. Properties are reported separately rather than pooled, which is the first place a set of books helps or gets in the way.

A rental belongs on Schedule C instead where significant services are provided to the renter — hotel-like services to the occupant rather than the space. Heat, water, trash collection, cleaning common areas and fixing what breaks are services to the space. Daily housekeeping, linens changed mid-stay and meals are services to the person. The distinction is not cosmetic: rent on Schedule E is generally outside self-employment tax; business income on Schedule C is not.

Short-term rentals

A cleaner between guests is maintenance of the space, and a furnished unit let by the week ordinarily still sits on Schedule E.

Depreciation, and the split you only make once

Residential rental property is written off over 27.5 years under the general depreciation system, using the mid-month convention: whatever day of the month it was placed in service, it is treated as placed in service mid-month.

Land is not depreciable. It does not wear out and does not become obsolete. So the purchase price has to be apportioned between land and building, and only the building side produces a deduction.

That apportionment is the most consequential number a landlord ever sets, and it is very often done carelessly — a round percentage chosen because it looked about right. It then follows the property for decades, setting every year’s depreciation, the adjusted basis, and the gain when the property finally sells. A defensible split has something behind it: the assessed land and building values on the town’s property card, an appraisal, an allocation stated in the closing documents. An hour spent on it in year one beats a week reconstructing it in year twenty.

Passive losses, the $25,000 allowance, and what happens to the rest

Rental real estate is a passive activity, and as a general rule passive losses do not offset wages. The exception most landlords rely on is a special allowance of up to $25,000 of rental loss against non-passive income where the taxpayer actively participates — approving tenants, setting rents, approving expenditures. That is a lower bar than material participation, and most owners of a three-family meet it.

The allowance then tapers: reduced by 50% of modified AGI above $100,000, fully phased out at $150,000.

A disallowed loss is suspended, not destroyed. It carries forward, and becomes available against passive income later or on a fully taxable disposition of the property. The risk is the record keeping — an owner told the loss did nothing this year stops tracking it, and then it is not there in the year it is worth most.

Real estate professional status

The status is claimed far more often than it is met. Both tests have to be satisfied in the same year: more than half of the personal services performed during the year in real property trades or businesses in which the taxpayer materially participates, and more than 750 hours of service in those trades or businesses. Someone working two thousand hours at a job elsewhere cannot satisfy the first test, whatever the second says.

The hours also have to be supported by a log kept as the year goes along, not a figure assembled from memory in March.

Repairs against improvements

This is the single most common source of a wrong landlord return. A repair keeps the property in ordinary operating condition and is deducted in the year it is paid. An improvement is capitalised and depreciated on its own clock. An expenditure is an improvement if it is a Betterment, an Adaptation, or a Restoration — the BAR framework.

BettermentCorrects a defect that existed at acquisition, or materially adds to capacity, quality or strength.Capitalise
AdaptationPuts the property to a use that was not its intended use when it was acquired.Capitalise
RestorationRebuilds the property to like-new condition, replaces a major component, or returns it to working order after deterioration.Capitalise

Patching a roof after a storm is a repair. A new roof is a restoration. Painting between tenants is a repair. Taking the kitchen back to the studs is a betterment. One week’s spending on one building routinely lands on both sides of the line.

Selling: the bill people forget is coming

Section 1250 & 1031

Depreciation is not a gift. The deductions taken over the holding period come back on sale as unrecaptured section 1250 gain, taxed at a maximum rate of 25% — above the rate on the rest of the long-term gain. Owners model a sale by subtracting what they paid from what they got, but adjusted basis is lower than the purchase price by every year of depreciation claimed, and the recapture arrives whether or not the proceeds are spent.

A like-kind exchange under section 1031 defers the gain, and now applies to real property only. The deadlines are the whole of it. They run in calendar days from the date the relinquished property transfers, and they run together — the 180 days is not 180 days after the 45.

Massachusetts taxes rental income at the flat 5% rate, with a further 4% surtax on taxable income above the threshold that is adjusted each year — which a sale year can reach, so once the federal treatment is settled the state consequence is straightforward to work out.

Short-term rentals are a separate regime. An Airbnb-type letting carries a state excise of 5.7%, plus whatever local option the municipality has adopted, plus registration with the Department of Revenue. Registration is a step of its own, unrelated to the income tax return, and the one most often missed by an owner who listed a unit on a platform and assumed the platform handled everything.

Books that hold up

Scott’s primary clientele for a decade, alongside quick-serve restaurant owners, were rental real estate owners. Most of the difficulty in a landlord’s return is not the law. It is that the records were kept as one pot of money and have to be taken apart afterwards.

  • Accounts kept per unit rather than per portfolio. Schedule E asks per property, and it is the only way to tell which building is actually earning.
  • Security deposits treated as a liability, not income. The money is held for the tenant, and becomes income only when applied to rent or kept against damage.
  • Capital improvements scheduled as they happen — date, cost, what was done, when it went into service. Reconstructing twenty years of them from a box of receipts at closing is the worst hour of a sale.
  • The mortgage statement split properly. Principal is not deductible; interest is.

Ordinary bookkeeping work, monthly or quarterly, makes April a matter of assembling rather than investigating.
