Scott Marchlik

Founder

Scott studied accounting at the University of Massachusetts, Lowell, and graduated in the spring of 2007 with a bachelor’s degree in business administration, concentration in accounting. Lowell was where he learned the subject and it is where the office sits now, a mile or so from the campus he walked out of.

In the summer of 2007 he took a job at a CPA firm in Cambridge as a staff accountant. He started on the bookkeeping side — posting, reconciling, closing months, chasing the entries that did not agree — and worked his way from there into business and individual tax preparation. He stayed for a decade. By the time he left in the spring of 2018 he was an accounting supervisor, and he left to open Mill City Accounting Services on his own.

One firm, one decade, two client types learned properly. That is the whole of it. Scott is an accountant, and what stands behind that is the work rather than a set of letters. What is here instead is a decade of returns filed, books closed and payrolls run for a narrow set of businesses, and a telephone that he answers himself.

Two kinds of client, learned over a decade

At the Cambridge firm Scott’s primary clientele were quick-serve restaurant owners, along with rental real estate owners. Those are the same two specialties set out on this site, and they are not a marketing choice. They are simply where he spent the first decade of his working life.

It matters because both are trades with their own habits. A quick-serve restaurant lives on daily sales summaries, tip reporting, meals tax, food and labor percentages, and a payroll that runs whether the week was good or not. Rental real estate lives on the basis of each building, the split between a repair and an improvement, depreciation schedules that must be carried forward without error for decades, and the passive loss rules that decide what a Schedule E loss is actually worth in a given year. Neither is difficult once you have done a few hundred of them. Both go wrong quietly when handled by someone who has not.

If you own one of the two, there is a page written for you.

  • Quick-serve restaurants — sales, tips, meals tax, payroll and the monthly close.
  • Rental real estate — basis, depreciation, repairs against improvements, Schedule E.

What a one-person firm means

The person who answers the telephone is the person who does the work. A return is not passed to a junior for a first draft and then initialled by someone who has never met you. There is no intake team, no account manager between you and the file, and no chance that the January conversation is forgotten by the following January, because there is only one memory to keep it in.

That is a real advantage and it has a real cost, and it is fairer to state both. A larger firm has depth of staff and can put four people on a problem in a week. Scott cannot. What he can do is know your file, and know it in March without opening it. If your business needs a large firm, you should engage one.

The practical effect is on the ordinary things. A question about whether a repair on a triple-decker is deductible this year gets answered by the person who set up the depreciation schedule in the first place. A payroll query in the middle of a Thursday lunch rush goes to the person who filed the last three quarterly returns.

What Scott does not do

He does not give legal advice, sell investments, or draft anything that belongs to an attorney. Where a question is properly a lawyer’s, he will say so rather than answer it. The new business work covers formation, operating agreements, annual reports and tax identification numbers — the filing side of standing a company up, not the litigation side.

The one license on this site

Notary Public

Scott is a notary public, licensed in the Commonwealth of Massachusetts. A Massachusetts notary is commissioned by the Governor for a seven-year term. The maximum fee for an acknowledgment is $1.25. Chapter 2 of the Acts of 2023 authorized electronic and remote online notarization in Massachusetts on a permanent basis.

It is a small thing, but it saves a trip when a deed, an affidavit or a lender’s package needs a seal in the same visit as the rest of the work. Details are on the notarization page.
