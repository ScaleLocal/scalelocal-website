How do you write a service page that ranks?

8 min read

A service page ranks when it covers one specific service for one specific area in genuine depth — the actual problem, your process, what it costs, and proof you’ve done it. Skip the keyword-stuffed fluff. Write for the homeowner who’s about to call someone, answer the questions they’re Googling, and you’ll rank and convert at the same time.

Let’s start with what doesn’t rank: a single “Services” page listing nineteen things you do in a bulleted column, each with one vague sentence. Google sees that as thin and unfocused, and homeowners see it as a company that does everything and specializes in nothing. With 46% of all searches carrying local intent, the contractors who win build real, dedicated pages — and the ones who don’t stay invisible.

Here’s the architecture and the writing formula that actually moves you up.

One page per service. One page per town.

The foundational rule: each distinct service gets its own page, and each town you serve gets its own page. A roofer doesn’t have a “Roofing” page — they have “Roof Replacement,” “Roof Repair,” “Storm Damage Restoration,” and a page for each major town in their service area.

Why? Because that’s how people search. Nobody types “roofing services.” They type “roof repair [their town]” or “storm damage roof [their town].” A focused page targeting that exact phrase beats a catch-all page every single time. This is also how you climb in the surrounding towns instead of only ranking where your pin sits — we cover the mechanics in why your business isn’t showing up on Google.

One hard rule: do not spin up clone pages with the town name swapped and nothing else changed. Google flags that as doorway-page spam. Each page needs genuinely different, local content.

The structure that ranks and converts

A strong service page follows a logical order that mirrors how a worried homeowner thinks:

  • A clear, specific H1. “Emergency Roof Repair in [Town]” — not “Welcome to Our Roofing Page.”
  • The problem, named. Open by describing the exact situation the reader is in. A homeowner with a ceiling stain wants to know you understand their problem before they care about your company.
  • Your process, step by step. What happens after they call? Inspection, estimate, timeline, cleanup. Removing uncertainty removes hesitation.
  • What it costs (or what drives the cost). Most contractors dodge this. Don’t. Even a range or “here’s what affects the price” builds trust and pre-filters the bargain hunters before they waste your time.
  • Proof. Photos of that exact service, two or three relevant reviews, your license and insurance.
  • One obvious next step. A visible phone number and a single clear call to action.

Write for the human, optimize for the robot second

Here’s where contractors get it backwards. They cram a keyword in twelve times and write robotic, lifeless copy. Google’s gotten far too smart for that, and it reads terribly. Write the page for the homeowner who’s about to call someone today. Use the keyword naturally in the H1, the first paragraph, and a subheading or two — then forget about it and just answer their questions clearly.

The best service pages quietly answer the questions buyers Google before they call: How long does it take? Will my insurance cover it? Do you clean up? How soon can you come out? Answer those, and you rank for dozens of long-tail searches you never even targeted — while reading like a human wrote it.

The details that tip it over the edge

  • Length with substance. Aim for genuine depth — enough to fully answer the question — not padding. A focused 700-word page beats a bloated 1,500-word one.
  • Real local references. Mention neighborhoods, common local issues (clay soil, coastal salt, hard winters), and projects you’ve actually done nearby.
  • Fast and mobile-first. Most of these searches happen on a phone. A slow page loses the ranking and the reader.
  • Internal links. Link related services to each other and back to your main pages. It helps Google understand your site and keeps readers moving toward a call.

Why this is worth the effort

A great service page is the asset that keeps paying. Unlike a paid ad that dies the second you stop funding it, a page that ranks brings in calls month after month for free. That’s why the rule of thumb for most contractors under $20k/month is roughly 80% effort on organic content that compounds, 20% on paid ads for high-intent emergency keywords. Service pages are the compounding 80%.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a service page be?

Long enough to fully answer the homeowner's questions and no longer — substance over word count. A focused 700-word page that covers the problem, your process, pricing factors, and proof beats a padded 1,500-word page every time. Pad it with fluff and both Google and the reader lose interest.

Should I have one page for all my services or separate pages?

Separate pages, always. Each distinct service should get its own page, and each town you serve should get one too, because that mirrors how people actually search. A focused page targeting 'roof repair in [town]' outranks a catch-all 'Services' page, since Google rewards depth and specificity over a thin bulleted list.

Can I just copy a service page and swap the town name?

No — Google flags near-duplicate clone pages as doorway-page spam and can penalize you for it. Each town page needs genuinely different, local content: real neighborhoods, local conditions, and projects you have actually done nearby. The effort is the point; it is what makes the page rank and feel credible.

How many keywords should I put on a service page?

Far fewer than you think. Use your main keyword naturally in the H1, the opening paragraph, and a subheading or two, then write for the human. Stuffing the same phrase a dozen times reads terribly and Google sees right through it. Answering the real questions buyers Google ranks you for far more terms anyway.
